PERSONAL ESSAY · 2026

Something
More.

How a 16-year-old in Istanbul built an archive about the World Trade Center and September 11.

The Beginning

I'm Kaan. I'm an independent researcher. I have been studying the World Trade Center.

When I saw the first 9/11 clip at home, I felt shocked. I couldn't believe it. The planes hit the skyscrapers. It was unreal. I told myself, "When I wake up, it will just be a dream, and the Twin Towers will still be there." It was just 3–4 seconds, but I felt relaxed and good. Another shocking event was the people who had to jump. I felt very sad.

Part of me was curious, but another part of me was scared too. I closed the video. Then I opened it again. I closed it and opened it again. Then I watched another video, and another, and another. Finally, I decided that I could make something for the people who died that day.

I want one thing: please don't forget 9/11. It matters. People didn't deserve to die.

From Research to an Archive

I watched a lot of videos, and I watched those moments again and again. I was telling my friend because he was learning. I didn't want to forget, and I was afraid that I would forget, so I wrote everything down. I wanted to be like the people who wrote FBI documents. I wrote the whole story about the construction, collapse, and aftermath.

My first draft was 266 pages. I showed it to my uncle during a holiday, and he said, "If you have a product, go sell it." But that was not okay for me because 9/11 was not something to sell. It was something more.

Instead, I made something useful for people. I looked for and researched sources from NIST, the FBI, and the Port Authority. Then I checked the sources with ChatGPT. I learned to use academic language. This changed me the most.

Reaching the People Behind History

I discovered Gmail, and then I started sending emails to 9/11 survivors. I wrote to a lot of people, but the first person to reply was Michael Hingson. I was so excited. I was very touched by his response. In his testimony, he described a moment when a voice told him not to worry about what he couldn't control. It was one of the most powerful things I read in my two years of research.

I added his testimony to my archive without changing a word, under his real name. He also recommended his books, and I added his books to my sources.

This was a turning point for me. I felt like a serious researcher, and I thought my archive had reached a higher level.

Later, I also contacted Janice Brooks and Professor Snyder. They are really good people. They took me seriously and contributed to a project that could be useful for me and many other people. This made me really happy, and these experiences made me even more excited about my research.

Being Taken Seriously

After I completed my archive, I started sending it to universities, libraries, and different institutions. I received some really nice responses from certain institutions, while many others never replied. Some institutions told me that they could not accept my archive as a donation, but they directed me to other institutions and opportunities. It made me really happy that they took the time to guide me. I only received an automatic reply from the Smithsonian, but this did not stop me. Instead, it motivated me to reach out to even more institutions.

Even though I was young, I was really excited that institutions were taking my work seriously. Columbia University became especially important to me. An archivist there reviewed my collection and described it as "small but mighty." Those words made me feel really good. The archivist always made time for me, carefully answered my questions, and gave me honest advice. I was very touched that someone would offer so much support to a 16-year-old writing from Istanbul. Writing to this archivist also taught me the importance of honesty. When I learned that my collection had been accepted, I was really emotional, and I felt different. When I looked back at everything I had done, I said to myself, "Wow."

One of the challenges I faced during this process was preparing my archive in academic language. I tried to choose my words carefully because I did not want to hurt anyone. Even after all these experiences, I still see myself as a rookie. I believe I have a lot to learn.

What Two Years Taught Me

Before I started this research, I never thought I could contact a survivor like Michael Hingson or an archivist at Columbia University. It still feels unbelievable that I have achieved these things. Sometimes, it feels like everything that happened to me is not real.

During this process, I learned how to do research and how to communicate with people. Before, I didn't even know how to send emails to people I didn't know, academics, or important institutions. Now, I think I'm good at it. The way I see myself has changed too. Now I believe I can achieve things that I once thought I couldn't do.

Of course, this process wasn't easy. Moving my project from Turkish to English was especially hard for me. I had many difficulties with vocabulary and academic language. It was also hard to write correct sentences and explain my research in English without losing its meaning. But I kept going.

Today, when I look at my archive, the people I have contacted, and my own progress, I feel proud of all of them.

The Responsibility of Remembering

I know I cannot change what happened that day. But I can remember it and help other people remember those who died that day. They had dreams, plans, and people who loved them. They had their own lives. They deserved to come home that day.

For me, remembering 9/11 is not only about remembering something that happened in the past. It is also about sharing these people's stories with future generations. In Turkey, we have a saying: "Those who do not know their past cannot shape their future." I think this saying is also very meaningful when it comes to remembering 9/11. We must show that the people who lost their lives did not die in vain. We must keep their stories alive and learn from what happened so we can build a better future. We must also pass this responsibility on to future generations. They should not only learn from what we tell them. They should also do their own research and learn about this history. Maybe one day, even if everyone forgets, at least one person will continue researching and reminding others.

I would really like my archive to reach the families of the 9/11 victims or survivors one day. I think it could mean a lot to them that someone from Turkey is interested in this event and researches their stories. Maybe they would feel that there are people who still remember them and the people they lost. I believe younger generations should learn about 9/11 not only as a historical event, but also as a lesson about human life, empathy, and responsibility.

I also want my archive to show that the World Trade Center should not only be remembered for its destruction. It was once a special place where people worked, spent time, went to concerts, and made beautiful memories. Its beautiful architecture and the life around it should also be remembered. Maybe because I never had the chance to live during those years, I am so interested in learning about that time, the people, and what the World Trade Center was like before 9/11. The story of the World Trade Center is not only about its destruction. The lives people lived there are also part of its history.

Finally, I believe that terrorism has no religion or nationality. Terrorism is terrorism. We must stand against terrorism, regardless of people's identities, and remember the innocent people who lost their lives.

AI, Transparency and My Own Work

During my research, I used artificial intelligence to help me with both research and writing. It showed me what I could improve academically, what sources I could find, which institutions I could send my archive to, and where I made mistakes in my writing. I also got help with images and sources. However, I did not just accept the information it gave me. I usually asked two different AI tools about the same information and checked it myself too. I especially used sources such as NIST and the Port Authority to check whether the information was correct.

I believe the archive is mostly my own work because I decided on the topic and scope of the research. I researched sources, contacted people, and worked to improve the archive. AI guided me, helped me see my mistakes, and helped me improve. When I was preparing the first draft, I also used AI for both research and writing. I think I would have struggled much more without AI. Maybe I would have even given up.

One of the most important rules for me was that AI should never change the testimonies of survivors or other people in any way. This is because testimonies are personal. It is very important for people to tell their own experiences in their own words. Hearing a survivor's story directly from them or reading it in writing is important for keeping the meaning and accuracy of their words and preserving history. For this reason, I added the testimonies I received to my archive without changing a single word.

I was also open about my use of AI when communicating with institutions. The archivist at Columbia University had some concerns about the use of AI. However, after considering my transparency about this, the archivist made an exception for my collection. This experience showed me how important it is to be honest and open.

I also used AI while writing this article. I wrote some sections in English myself and only asked AI to show me my mistakes. I wrote other sections in Turkish and got help from AI to translate them into English. However, all the ideas and experiences in this article are my own.

I have one piece of advice for young people who want to do research in the future: Don't let AI do all the research for you. Do the research yourself, see your mistakes, and learn what you can improve. Use AI not as a tool that does everything for you, but as a tool to help you improve yourself. I believe the important thing is to learn and grow with AI.

Looking Back

When I look back at my two years of research, the things that make me most proud are the archive itself, the people I have contacted, and the changes I have seen in myself during this process. If the Kaan of 2024 could see me today, I think he would be most surprised that I could do so many things for two years without getting bored. During this process, I became someone who can communicate with people and express himself, but who is still learning.

If I could give just one piece of advice to the person I was in 2024, when I had just started this research, I would say: "Keep going and do it." These two years showed me what I am capable of and helped me believe that I can do even bigger projects. In the future, I plan to visit the 9/11 Memorial Museum and prepare a completely new research project based on what I see and learn there. I want to decide on the topic of that research when I visit the museum.

I hope my archive will be remembered years from now as a project that people can benefit from. I hope it will help people understand that they should protect each other and guide their future better by learning from the past. I also want young people who read this article to do in-depth research on the subjects they love. If they are interested in something, they should not be satisfied with basic information. They should keep learning.

This project is not the end for me. It is the beginning of new projects. What I want to say to myself and everyone who wants to start doing research is this: Don't stop. Keep going. Persevere.
