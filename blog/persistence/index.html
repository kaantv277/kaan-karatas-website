Persist until
you succeed.

Why I kept going when the work was tedious, and what persistence has meant for my archive.

From my book in progress, The Guide I Needed · Chapter 6

You’re right: there are times when you get tired. It happens to me, too. Sometimes you know what you want to do, you know why it matters, and you even know your next step. But you still don’t feel like doing it.

Taking the easy way out always feels easier. You tell yourself you can continue tomorrow, that you deserve a break, or that maybe the whole thing isn’t worth it. Sometimes a break is exactly what you need. But sometimes you are not really looking for rest; you are looking for a way out of something that has become hard or tedious.

I recently felt that kind of tiredness myself. Then I remembered an idea from James Clear’s Atomic Habits: talent and luck can shape your results, but what you put in again and again matters too. That doesn’t mean you should ignore exhaustion or work every minute of the day. It means you shouldn’t let one hard day make the decision for your whole future.

I learned this while building my World Trade Center Archive.

Failure Is Part of the Process

The hardest part of the whole project was translating the archive into English. I struggled with it so much that I almost gave up.

At first, I couldn’t translate it properly. So I tried the easy way and let AI do it. It seemed like a smart shortcut: why spend so much time doing something by hand if a tool can do it for me?

But the result was not what I needed. The AI damaged the images and mixed up the text. Instead of making things easier, it created new problems, and I couldn’t just take the result and move on.

That taught me something: using a tool is not the same as doing the work. AI could help me, but it couldn’t take responsibility for the quality of my archive. I still had to make sure everything was accurate, readable and in the right order.

There was another problem: the size of the project. My first drafts were 266–366 pages long. The current version is a tighter 100 pages. Creating something is not only about adding more. Sometimes you have to go back, question what you have done, cut what isn’t needed and make hard decisions.

I could have treated all of this as a reason to stop. I didn’t. I kept working.

And here is something I want you to notice: most of what you are doing usually isn’t hard. It’s tedious, or you haven’t fully understood it yet. You know what needs to be done; you just don’t want to do it again. Check one more detail. Fix one more mistake. Review one more page. That is where persistence matters. You don’t need a dramatic moment or a sudden wave of motivation. You just need to come back to the work and finish the next task.

Persisting is why my archive got a news story on Turk of America, and why the donation process with Columbia University’s oral history archives is now underway. None of that happened because everything went smoothly. It happened after mistakes, rewrites and a lot of continued effort. And I am still learning.

I Will Persist Until I Succeed

I first heard this idea in Napoleon Hill’s Think and Grow Rich, and then in Og Mandino’s The Greatest Salesman in the World:

“I WILL PERSIST UNTIL I SUCCEED.”

Please hang this sentence somewhere you can see it every day. You don’t have to feel motivated every day or know exactly how everything will work out. But you do need to remember why you started.

I also want to be honest with you: persistence doesn’t guarantee success. You can work hard and still fail. Sometimes your plan is wrong, and sometimes your approach has to change. Persistence is not repeating the same mistake again and again. It is learning from what went wrong, changing your approach and trying again when the goal is still worth it.

Think about my translation story. The answer was not to keep using the broken result. I had to see the problem and keep working toward a better version.

Remember Why You Started

There will be days when you look at how much work is left and feel nowhere near the finish line. When that happens, remember why you started. For me, one reason is that I want to buy a house or a car for my mom and dad one day. Your reason will be your own. Do you want to learn a language, build something of your own or create a life that looks different from the one you have now? Whatever your reason is, don’t forget it when the process gets boring.

Then ask yourself:

  • Why did I start this?
  • Does this goal still matter to me?
  • What have I learned so far?
  • What is the next small step I can take?

Don’t confuse a slow process with a pointless one. You are allowed to rest, and you are allowed to change direction. But don’t confuse one bad day with proof that you can’t do it. The next time you feel like giving up, don’t ask yourself whether you can finish the whole journey. Ask yourself whether you can take the next step.

Your Exercise: The Persistence Check

Think of one goal or project that matters to you but that you have been struggling to continue. Write down your answers.

  1. What did you start? Describe it in one or two sentences.
  2. Why did you start? What made it important to you?
  3. What is stopping you? Be honest: time, fear, boredom, not knowing how, or something else?
  4. What have you learned from your earlier attempts? Write one mistake and what you would do differently.
  5. What is your next small action? Choose one task you can really finish in the next 48 hours.

Don’t write a huge promise to yourself. Write something you can actually do. Then do it.

PERSIST UNTIL YOU SUCCEED.

This is a chapter from my book in progress, The Guide I Needed: A Teenager’s Guide to Finding Your Direction and Taking Action. The exercise questions are meant to be answered on paper.
