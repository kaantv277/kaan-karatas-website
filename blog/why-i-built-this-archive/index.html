Why I built
this archive.

How a building becomes a symbol, and why history belongs to anyone willing to take it seriously.

This archive began with a question I could not stop asking: how does a building become a symbol?

I am Kaan Karataş, a sixteen-year-old student from Istanbul, Türkiye. I have no institutional affiliation, no university degree, and no formal background in history or architecture. What I have is two years of independent research, conducted after school and on weekends, driven by the conviction that the World Trade Center deserved to be documented, not as a tragedy alone, but as a place that existed, that mattered, and that was full of life before it was destroyed.

This archive is a documentary history of the World Trade Center, covering its origins, its architecture, the 1993 bombing, the events of September 11, 2001, and the reconstruction of the site. It draws on official government reports, engineering investigations, public photographic archives, and published accounts. I have tried to ground every claim in a source that the reader can check, and to say so plainly where a claim is disputed or where I could not verify it.

What transformed this project into something I did not anticipate came in June 2026, when I began reaching out to people who had lived through the events described in these pages. Michael Hingson, who walked down from the 78th floor of the North Tower on September 11, 2001, with his guide dog Roselle, contributed a written testimony. Janice Brooks, who was on the 84th floor of the South Tower that morning, contributed a detailed written account of her escape. Robert W. Snyder, Professor Emeritus of American Studies and Journalism at Rutgers University and Manhattan Borough Historian from 2019 to 2025, recorded an oral reflection on that day and on how the city has remembered it. Their contributions, reproduced in Appendix B, are voices that no official report can fully capture.

This is the second major version of the archive. In preparing it I replaced every photograph whose source or license I could not establish with images from public archives, rewrote most of the text against its sources, and rebuilt the bibliography. I used AI tools to help draft, check, and format the text; their role is described in the section on methodology. The testimonies are the words of their authors.

The archive has been submitted to a number of libraries and institutions, which are listed in the Archive Record. Their inclusion there records only that the archive was sent to them.

This archive is presented as an act of independent scholarship, a term that carries both a claim and a limitation. It claims the right to conduct serious historical research outside of institutional structures. It acknowledges that such research, by its nature, lacks the peer review, editorial oversight, and institutional verification that formal academic publication provides. The reader is invited to evaluate the evidence and methodology presented here on their own terms.

I did not write this document because I was asked to. I wrote it because I believed it was worth writing, and because I hoped it might, in some small way, contribute to the preservation of a history that belongs not only to New York, but to the world. History is not the exclusive property of institutions. It belongs to anyone willing to take it seriously. This archive is my attempt to do exactly that.

Kaan Karataş
Istanbul, October 2026

This text is the foreword to the World Trade Center Archive, Version 2.2. The full archive is open access on Zenodo: doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24566263.
